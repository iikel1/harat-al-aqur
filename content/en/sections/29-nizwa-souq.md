Nizwa Souq is closely bound to Harat Al Aqur, the Friday Mosque and the pale fort. From of old, Nizwa Castle, and then the fort — the two of them the political seat of the state — were bound to bring into being a large principal souq for the region.

Nothing is known of the age of this souq except that it is among the most important Omani markets, past and present. For its long standing and its purchasing power, many proverbs were once coined about it. In **1992** it won an award for the best architectural and engineering design.

What distinguishes it at present is that most of its sellers are Omanis. It is a souq in which goods do not go unsold, however many and various they are, and it has room for everyone to work in it, the young before the old. **Friday morning** is the ideal time to visit, for its striking crowds and the variety of goods.

The most important of the small souqs belonging to Nizwa Souq, and the busiest and liveliest at present:

### 1 — The eastern souq (Al Sansara)

A souq supplying every kind of spice and seasoning and aromatic, traditional Omani remedies and local oils, together with some rare materials used in cooking, in farming and in treatment. It also holds a great many items that are now sold nowhere else, because so few people ask for them.

`[image]` *An old photograph of the Al Sansara souq from the western door*

### 2 — The silver and craftsmen's souq

Nizwa has been known since ancient times for working gold and silver, and keeps to it today. The silver shops are grouped near the entrance of Nizwa Fort, with another group beside the arms souq.

The most important thing these shops supply is the **Omani khanjar** in its various types — the *nizwani* and the *sa'idi* (the *safani*) — along with the materials for making and repairing them, and women's jewellery, traditional and modern, locally made and imported, in different forms. They also offer a making and repair service for whatever customers ask.

### 3 — The gold souq

It lies north of the fort and west of the Friday Mosque, in the form of two strips divided by the street. It is one of the most important gold markets in the Sultanate, serving every governorate and supplying the different settings a woman might need, local or imported.

### 4 — The arms souq

This means the traditional weapons whose trade is permitted in the Sultanate. The souq supplies these weapons together with ammunition and accessories, and a cleaning and maintenance service. **Every Friday morning a public auction** is held for some of these weapons, brought to the souq directly by their owners.

### 5 — The Friday market

An open souq for everyone, where you may offer whatever goods are permitted by law and by custom. What is offered most are cars, furniture, household vessels, incense and clothes. It begins in the first hours of Friday morning and runs to eleven o'clock.

### 6 — The dates and Omani halwa souq

Wilayat Nizwa is known for growing the finest date palms and for making Omani halwa. Its souq is therefore full of stalls selling the halwa served in Omani hospitality with dates and coffee. It also makes a fine gift, packed in handsome vessels that usually lean towards Omani tradition. There are also stalls selling fresh and dried dates and their by-products — *dibs*, *madluk* and *ma'mul*.

The souq further holds a great many of the goods an Omani household needs — meat, poultry, fish, vegetables, fruit, aromatics, clothes and the rest — and has kept up old traditional services, some of which have vanished from most markets. Examples:

- **The coppersmiths and blacksmiths** — who make and repair copper household vessels, and make and sharpen knives, swords and cleavers. - **The jewellers** — who repair gold, silver and *khanjars*. - **The cobbler (*iskafi*)** — who repairs and stitches shoes, and does leatherwork such as cutting sheaths for knives and some animal and agricultural requisites.

Beyond that are the various services now available in every city in the world — tailoring, barbering, dressmaking, watch and electronics repair and so on.

### 7 — The livestock calling

Nizwa Souq holds goods of every kind, and a large part of them is sold by communal auction on **Friday morning** of each week, and on the last days before Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha. Among these is the ground for selling sheep and cattle, known locally as the *habta* and the *munada*. This ground is the best known in the Sultanate and the most crowded.

Livestock is brought to it from across the wilayats of Oman, along with sellers coming in from the nearby villages. Cattle come in numbers from the Governorate of Dhofar, and the larger animals from the Governorate of North Al Batinah; a great many sheep come from the governorates of Al Sharqiyah, Al Dhahirah and Al Wusta.

The auction begins at **seven in the morning** with the sale of sheep, then cattle, and runs for about **three hours**, with dozens of brokers taking part at the same time. The result is a fine spectacle, sought out by many from the wilayat and beyond. It is an old custom in many of the principal Omani towns.
