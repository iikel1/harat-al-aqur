title: The Omani house in Harat Al Aqur

Typically, the ground floor of an Omani house in Harat Al Aqur holds a *majlis*, bedrooms, a passage (*dahliz*), a date store known as the *nadad*, a washroom then called the *shurūni*, and a private well — two houses sometimes sharing a single well.

The internal division of the upper floor varied with the wishes of the household, but in most of these houses part of the upper floor was open to the sky. The kitchen I most often found on the upper floor, and sometimes in a room set between the two floors on the stair.

Among the most important fittings of the house are the *muwaqqa'a*, a stone for pounding grain, and the place for making coffee, which stands in the guest room or the passage.

House sizes run from about **60 to 200 square metres**. Looking closely, one finds no very large or very lavish houses in Al Aqur of the kind found in some Omani villages, and there are several reasons for this. Although Al Aqur was the principal quarter in Oman, the character of its people ran to learning, work and farming; they had little interest in luxury in house-building — which of course did not stop them from fine engineering invention in how they built. The high density of population also left no room for houses on a large footprint.

The doors are carved wood, with handsome arches. The ceilings are palm trunks, decorated and carved, inscribed with dates, Qur'anic verses and lines of poetry. The windows are wooden with four openings, so the occupant could open the upper lights without the lower ones if he chose.

A house must have a jar (*jahla*) for drinking water, earthenware vessels, and chests (*manadis*) for clothes. On the walls are niches, shelves and hooks. The walls were made very thick to carry the weight above and to allow those niches to be cut. Some house walls carry painted decoration in green, red and white.
