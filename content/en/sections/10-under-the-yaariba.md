The greatest building renaissance the quarter has seen was probably in the age of this state. The construction of the pale fort within it was followed by many of the fortification works the quarter needed, among them the rebuilding and restoration of the Al Aqur Wall on its present line and plan.

Nizwa, like every Omani city, took its share of the refinement and prosperity of the Ya'ariba period — in its plains, its mountains, its villages and its farms — as a result of the strength on land and sea and the Omani revival that followed the expulsion of the Portuguese and the end of the Nabahina state.

In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries Oman came to face, alone, the greatest powers in the world across the Indian Ocean: the Portuguese, the English, the Dutch, the French and the Persians. Most of these states first sought to control Oman; when they could not, they established diplomatic and commercial relations with it and appointed ambassadors. Entering the Indian Ocean came to require the permission of the Omani fleet, whose ships patrolled it, and all these foreign powers and fleets came to fear a confrontation with Oman.

In learning, knowledge spread throughout Oman through the Imams' care for it — founding schools and paying for them — and through the sheer number of scholars. Writers excelled in their prose and their verse. It is entirely natural that the appearance of life should change in all its details: within the family itself, in the thinking of men and the talk of women, in the games of children and in how houses were built, and in the variety of goods in the markets.

There is no room for insularity or backwardness in a state that builds the greatest of castles and forts; that brings home cannon and gunpowder as spoils; that splits the solid rock beneath its own ground so that the *aflaj* may burst into their channels, its labourers neither fearing the pitch darkness nor pleading the depth; whose mountain people cut roads for themselves out of hard mountain, with stones of great weight quarried and carried into place, then turn the deaf rock into agricultural terraces and green oases; and whose people put out to sea for months at a time to trade, unafraid of raging surf or the dark of night — sometimes to confront an enemy preying on the weak, or to answer the call of the wronged and the plundered on the coast of Africa.

And because Harat Al Aqur was the political and religious arena of that age, it is the place that most fully lived the consequences of all this — indeed the first to do so. Any influence began its reflection here.
