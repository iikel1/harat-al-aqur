Omanis were accustomed to using the finest woods for house doors and windows, and for ceilings too. For ceilings they relied mostly on palm trunks, for their strength, their durability and their abundance. For doors and windows, besides some Omani timber, they used strong Indian woods brought to them by Omani merchants who crossed the seas east and west.

They would often record on the doors and ceilings the date of the work, the name of the craftsman, the name of the owner of the house, and the ruler of Oman at the time.

### A door inscription

Written on this door:

> My writing is on this smoothed and adorned door, > with six days remaining of Rabi' al-Akhir, > > in the year that follows eighty by three, > with a thousand to match it in the reckoning set down — > > in the reign of Sultan bin Saif bin Malik, > Imam of mankind, the victorious Ya'rubi, > > for the chief of judges, the open-handed Muhammad, > son of Ibn Ubaidan, the pure jurist, > > who put on the mantle of learning, forbearance and good sense, > … girded about with restraint. > > Its carpenter is Abd Al Ilah, brother of sound judgement, > descendant of Sinan, of renowned integrity.

*(The date given is Rabi' al-Akhir 1083 AH.)*

We find that the carpenter was extremely skilled and a master of verse. In these six lines he recorded information of real importance for the generations that followed: his own name, the name of the owner of the house, his office and his standing in learning, the date, and the name of the Imam.
