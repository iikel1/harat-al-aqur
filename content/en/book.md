## Harat Al Aqur

To speak of Harat Al Aqur is to speak of the history of Nizwa — and that means speaking
of the history of Oman. Nizwa was the capital of Oman through most of the Islamic
periods, and Harat Al Aqur was the seat of Omani rule. From it the decrees and the binding
decisions were issued; from it the great armies moved; from it instructions went out to
the Omani fleets in the Gulf and the Indian Ocean. It is beyond dispute one of the most
important old quarters in Oman.

To care about it is to care about Omani history, and to preserve it is the duty of every
citizen. It is the inheritance of the forefathers held for their descendants, a trust
passed from generation to generation. There is no good in one who squanders a trust, and
no worth in one who cannot recognise the price of an inheritance — and Oman's inheritance
is learning and history. Nizwa is the cradle of scholars and the seat of statesmen and of
wise men. I will not dwell on its glories: the pale fort *(al-Qal'a al-Shahba)* is hardly
invisible to the short-sighted, and the old souq is the best witness and trace of it.

A solid wall surrounds Harat Al Aqur, carrying **fifteen towers and four gates**. Its
houses number more than **three hundred**, built of clay, hard stone and *sarooj*. The
evidence within it shows it was inhabited before Islam. Inside are five old mosques and a
great many schools and *sablahs*. Narrow lanes and alleys run between its houses, and the
houses interlock with one another because of how many people lived in them and how many
came to them. It holds many tribes — indeed most of the well-known Omani tribes are
present in it. Its gates were shut at night, and after they closed there was no way in.

This book speaks only of Harat Al Aqur inside the wall. The quarter's original boundaries
ran west to the cemetery of the Imams (the Al Ain district), south to Ghaf Al Sheikh, and
north and east to the bed of the wadi. Those outer areas hold a great deal worth
visiting: many graves of Omani Imams, of their leading scholars and of their notables,
and a wealth of towers, mosques, *aflaj* and other historic remains. I have kept to this
one place so as not to go on at length, or stray from the purpose this work was made for.
In the pages that follow I set out the most important known sites within it.

## Nizwa Fort — Al Qal'a Al Shahba

This is one of the most important and most visited places in Oman — indeed a principal
destination for every visitor to the Sultanate in general and to Nizwa in particular. It
is the pride of the Ya'ariba state and a crown of the great Omani history. Architecturally
it is a work of rare craft: its form, its entrances, its cannon, its wells, and its
methods of defence.

As for how the building was paid for: after Imam **Sultan bin Saif Al Ya'rubi** drove the
Portuguese occupier out of Oman in **1650**, he pursued them into the Arabian Gulf and
into every place they were found in the Indian Ocean — above all in India and East
Africa. At the city of **Diu**, near Mumbai in India, he mounted three severe campaigns,
enough to weaken what was then the strongest empire in the world and to make it taste
defeat after defeat. By this the Omani fleet took a great quantity of spoils, and with
them the Imam built fortifications and agricultural works across Oman — chief among them
this pale fort.

Work on it took **twelve years**. Omani *sarooj* was used in its construction, and its
base is strengthened with large, strong stones brought from the mountains. That is why the
fort was barely touched — only very slightly — when it was shelled by British air-force
rockets. It has many kinds of defence that make an enemy incapable of climbing it, and
that a visitor cannot understand without a guide. It has secret passages and remarkable
concealments, and it holds a great many cannon and weapons, much of it taken as spoils of
war.

## Nizwa Castle

Construction of Nizwa Castle began in the second century AH, some twelve hundred years
ago, in the time of Imam **Muhammad bin Abdullah bin Abi Affan**. Additions followed in
the time of Imam **Al Salt bin Malik Al Kharusi** and of the Imams who came after him.
The largest additions were probably made under Imam Al Salt bin Malik, coinciding with
Oman's strength on land and at sea and with the great fleet the Imam sent to the relief of
the people of **Socotra**, oppressed by the Christians, after a woman named Fatima
Al Suqutriya appealed to him in a famous ode setting out what they had done.

With that same fleet the Imam also cleared the Indian Ocean of pirates, after the emperor
of India complained to him of their attacks on merchants and their plundering of shipping
— for which the emperor presented Imam Al Salt with a precious Indian sword.

The castle is counted among the grand Omani fortifications. It contains rooms, wells,
stores, prisons, rooms for teaching and for provisions, and other chambers besides. Nizwa
Fort was later built directly alongside it.

## Al Shawadhna Mosque

This is the second mosque built in Oman, raised in the ninth year of the Hijra. The age of
its foundation shows that Harat Al Aqur was then among the most important places in Oman,
and that its people entered Islam when the Prophet's call reached them — perhaps even
before that.

The mosque is distinguished by an exceptionally fine *mihrab*, in which the carver
excelled, making it a model of Omani Islamic ornament. It was made in the year **936 AH**,
and — as was the Omani habit — the craftsman recorded his name on the carving in a
beautiful hand.

The mosque has undergone many restorations since it was first built more than fourteen
hundred years ago, the most recent in **2003** by the Omani Ministry of Heritage. A great
many scholars came out of it; some of them would keep vigil in it between the evening and
dawn prayers, for study and recitation.

## Mazara'a Mosque

One of the old mosques of Harat Al Aqur, its construction going back to the third century
AH. It stands on a raised platform, as old mosques do. Its *mihrab* carries a Qur'anic
verse.

This is among the most spacious mosques in Harat Al Aqur. Its roof bears the dates of old
ceiling works, and its walls carry inscriptions recording various periods, collected by
Ustadh Muhammad bin Abdullah Al Saifi in his book *Al Hulal Al Sundusiyya fi'l-Kitabat
Al Masjidiyya*.

It was restored again in **2020**, paid for out of its own endowment — it is among the
mosques rich in *waqf*. Its trustees took care to restore it in clay, *sarooj*, stone and
timber. The quarter has previously lost a great many important historic buildings, pulled
down to be rebuilt in modern materials — the most significant of them the Friday Mosque of
Nizwa and Al Sheikh Mosque, meaning that of Sheikh Bashir bin Al Mundhir.

### The story of its first building

The story of this mosque's first construction is that a man paid for it without anyone
knowing: he would send the daily wages to the builders. God willed that this good man
should die before the work was finished, and people asked why the building had stopped. A
man went to the foreman, who told him there had been someone paying the wages, that God
had taken him, and that he had asked for his name not to be mentioned. The man said to the
foreman: finish the building — I will cover the rest, and do not mention my name either.

So God willed that two men in succession should build this mosque, and the names of
neither are known. The mosque is still full of worshippers to this day.

## The Friday Mosque of Nizwa and the Islam Information Centre

One of the well-known Omani Friday mosques, built on the bank of **Wadi Kalbuh** in the
second century AH, around twelve hundred years ago. It lies to the north-east of Nizwa
Fort and is among the oldest congregational mosques, with extensive endowments. Its
administration passed to the Diwan of the Royal Court, and it was rebuilt at the beginning
of the renaissance of Sultan Qaboos bin Said.

In it the scholars used to gather to examine questions of learning and of politics, to
install Imams, and to settle important matters. From its pulpit the preachers spoke —
scholars, Imams and men of piety — across the ages of Oman's great history. It contains
halls and classrooms for students and a large public library holding every branch of
learning.

An **Islam Information Centre** has recently been added, in response to the number of
non-Muslims wishing to understand the concepts of Islam and its tolerance. A second such
centre is currently being built west of Mazara'a Mosque, under the Ministry of Endowments
and Religious Affairs.

## Nizwa Heritage Inn

Nizwa Heritage Inn was opened under the patronage of His Excellency Sheikh Hamad bin Salim
bin Saif Al Aghbari, Wali of Nizwa, on **1 Dhu al-Qa'da 1439 AH, corresponding to 23 April
2018**.

The company had undertaken this initiative from its founding, to turn the quarter into a
tourist destination, to preserve the heritage of the forefathers, to show later
generations how their ancestors lived, to encourage residents and house-owners to make
good use of the place, and to carry on the work of restoring every landmark in the quarter
— houses, schools, *sablahs*, towers and walls. That is the goal being sought.

This site holds a large place in the affections of the older generation, who used to meet
here to look over the news, to read, and to take coffee. That attachment shows in the
number of older people who come to it, and who then tell the stories and the anecdotes of
the quarter while they enjoy their coffee — living, for a while, as though they were in
the past.

## The Al Aqur Wall

Among the enduring works of Nizwa is the Al Aqur Wall, one of the venerable walls of Oman
and one of its important fortifications. It is genuinely a rare architectural piece, with
particular qualities and engineering features that set it apart from the other surviving
walls.

There had been an earlier wall, worn away, whose ruins remained visible; the old wall
appears to have been damaged by flooding of the wadis in the year **853 AH**. Its
construction is most likely to be dated to the time of Imam Al Salt bin Malik Al Kharusi.
Imam **Sultan bin Saif bin Malik Al Ya'rubi** then founded the Al Aqur Wall anew after its
ruin, giving it defined entrances and protective towers, and grandeur in its height and
width. On that basis the founding of the present Al Aqur Wall falls in the **second half
of the eleventh century AH**.

Building the wall took **seven years**. Imam Sultan built it at the same time as Nizwa
Fort, assigning the workmen to its construction. The wall has since passed through a
number of repairs and restorations. It surrounds Harat Al Aqur and runs to about **two
kilometres**. It is the most important line of defence for the people of the quarter.

Its thickness ranges from **one and a half to two metres**, with a rock base adding to its
solidity, and its height ranges from **five to six metres**. The wall carries a number of
towers attached to it — said to be **fifteen** — with **150 to 200 metres** between each
pair.

### The towers

Each tower has a name it is known by:

- **Al Mathbaha Tower** — in the northern corner, on the western side of the wall.
- **Mirza Tower** — on the southern side of Al Shajbi Mosque; some call it Bustan Qassam
  Tower.
- **Balj Tower** — on the eastern side, on the west side of Nizwa Souq.
- **Al Aliya Tower** — situated above Balj Tower.
- **Ghuwair Tower** — in the souq, facing the gate of the eastern souq known as
  Al Sansara. The wall used to be joined to this tower, and not free-standing as it
  appears today.
- **Al Souq Tower**, **Al Qal'a Tower**, **Bustan Al Aqur Tower** (first and second),
  **Al Sabkha Tower** (beside Sabah Al Sabkha), **Qat'at Al Tawi Tower**, **Harat
  Al Zama Tower**, **Sikkat Al Qabr Tower**, and **Kharis Balj Tower**.

Most of these towers are cylindrical, with circular bases four to five metres across;
others are cubic, with square bases. At every tower there is a stair connected to the
wall, so that soldiers could get up and down during their watches.

### The gates

The wall has four entrances or gates, the only ways in and out for the people of the
quarter. These entrances are known as *sabah*:

- **Sabah Abi Al Mu'thir** — on the southern side
- **Sabah Al Shajbi** — on the western side
- **Sabah Al Souq** — to the north-east
- **Sabah Al Sabkha** — on the eastern side

Each gate had a keeper who managed the traffic through it. **Sabah Al Souq was demolished**
during the restoration works on the fort and the souq, and nothing of it remains but some
photographs.

## The Omani house in Harat Al Aqur

Typically, the ground floor of an Omani house in Harat Al Aqur holds a *majlis*, bedrooms,
a passage (*dahliz*), a date store known as the *nadad*, a washroom then called the
*shurūni*, and a private well — two houses sometimes sharing a single well.

The internal division of the upper floor varied with the wishes of the household, but in
most of these houses part of the upper floor was open to the sky. The kitchen I most often
found on the upper floor, and sometimes in a room set between the two floors on the stair.

Among the most important fittings of the house are the *muwaqqa'a*, a stone for pounding
grain, and the place for making coffee, which stands in the guest room or the passage.

House sizes run from about **60 to 200 square metres**. Looking closely, one finds no very
large or very lavish houses in Al Aqur of the kind found in some Omani villages, and there
are several reasons for this. Although Al Aqur was the principal quarter in Oman, the
character of its people ran to learning, work and farming; they had little interest in
luxury in house-building — which of course did not stop them from fine engineering
invention in how they built. The high density of population also left no room for houses
on a large footprint.

The doors are carved wood, with handsome arches. The ceilings are palm trunks, decorated
and carved, inscribed with dates, Qur'anic verses and lines of poetry. The windows are
wooden with four openings, so the occupant could open the upper lights without the lower
ones if he chose.

A house must have a jar (*jahla*) for drinking water, earthenware vessels, and chests
(*manadis*) for clothes. On the walls are niches, shelves and hooks. The walls were made
very thick to carry the weight above and to allow those niches to be cut. Some house walls
carry painted decoration in green, red and white.

## Harat Al Aqur under the Ya'ariba

The greatest building renaissance the quarter has seen was probably in the age of this
state. The construction of the pale fort within it was followed by many of the
fortification works the quarter needed, among them the rebuilding and restoration of the
Al Aqur Wall on its present line and plan.

Nizwa, like every Omani city, took its share of the refinement and prosperity of the
Ya'ariba period — in its plains, its mountains, its villages and its farms — as a result
of the strength on land and sea and the Omani revival that followed the expulsion of the
Portuguese and the end of the Nabahina state.

In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries Oman came to face, alone, the greatest powers
in the world across the Indian Ocean: the Portuguese, the English, the Dutch, the French
and the Persians. Most of these states first sought to control Oman; when they could not,
they established diplomatic and commercial relations with it and appointed ambassadors.
Entering the Indian Ocean came to require the permission of the Omani fleet, whose ships
patrolled it, and all these foreign powers and fleets came to fear a confrontation with
Oman.

In learning, knowledge spread throughout Oman through the Imams' care for it — founding
schools and paying for them — and through the sheer number of scholars. Writers excelled
in their prose and their verse. It is entirely natural that the appearance of life should
change in all its details: within the family itself, in the thinking of men and the talk
of women, in the games of children and in how houses were built, and in the variety of
goods in the markets.

There is no room for insularity or backwardness in a state that builds the greatest of
castles and forts; that brings home cannon and gunpowder as spoils; that splits the solid
rock beneath its own ground so that the *aflaj* may burst into their channels, its
labourers neither fearing the pitch darkness nor pleading the depth; whose mountain people
cut roads for themselves out of hard mountain, with stones of great weight quarried and
carried into place, then turn the deaf rock into agricultural terraces and green oases;
and whose people put out to sea for months at a time to trade, unafraid of raging surf or
the dark of night — sometimes to confront an enemy preying on the weak, or to answer the
call of the wronged and the plundered on the coast of Africa.

And because Harat Al Aqur was the political and religious arena of that age, it is the
place that most fully lived the consequences of all this — indeed the first to do so. Any
influence began its reflection here.

## Doors and ceilings

Omanis were accustomed to using the finest woods for house doors and windows, and for
ceilings too. For ceilings they relied mostly on palm trunks, for their strength, their
durability and their abundance. For doors and windows, besides some Omani timber, they
used strong Indian woods brought to them by Omani merchants who crossed the seas east and
west.

They would often record on the doors and ceilings the date of the work, the name of the
craftsman, the name of the owner of the house, and the ruler of Oman at the time.

### A door inscription

Written on this door:

> My writing is on this smoothed and adorned door,
> with six days remaining of Rabi' al-Akhir,
>
> in the year that follows eighty by three,
> with a thousand to match it in the reckoning set down —
>
> in the reign of Sultan bin Saif bin Malik,
> Imam of mankind, the victorious Ya'rubi,
>
> for the chief of judges, the open-handed Muhammad,
> son of Ibn Ubaidan, the pure jurist,
>
> who put on the mantle of learning, forbearance and good sense,
> … girded about with restraint.
>
> Its carpenter is Abd Al Ilah, brother of sound judgement,
> descendant of Sinan, of renowned integrity.

*(The date given is Rabi' al-Akhir 1083 AH.)*

We find that the carpenter was extremely skilled and a master of verse. In these six lines
he recorded information of real importance for the generations that followed: his own
name, the name of the owner of the house, his office and his standing in learning, the
date, and the name of the Imam.

## Qur'an schools

Across most Omani quarters — as is still the case today — there were many schools for the
Qur'an, for the sciences of the language and of literature, and for some other subjects.
In Harat Al Aqur there is a group of such schools; parts of some of their walls survive,
and some have recently been restored. Endowments were assigned to them long ago.

Out of these small schools came a great many major scholars, who afterwards became men of
state with real standing. The size of the school never mattered as much as the learning
and the teacher.

## The *sablahs*

Harat Al Aqur is divided internally into five sub-quarters, and not one of them is without
a *majlis* or *sablah* where the residents gather regularly. There they exchange their
news, honour their guests and share their food — for their life was a social one, and
their neighbourliness was brotherly, bound by religion and by good conduct towards those
next door.

## Bait Al Sarooj

One of the largest houses in Harat Al Aqur, and among the grandest and most solid. Although
many of the houses in this quarter were built with *sarooj*, this house alone became known
by the name.

It was formerly lived in by a number of important political figures, among them **Sayyid
Tariq bin Taimur Al Busaidi**, father of Sultan Haitham bin Tariq — may God protect him.

## The emblem of Nizwa, Capital of Islamic Culture

Nizwa was chosen as **Capital of Islamic Culture for the year 2015**, for its great
standing in learning and for its many contributions to religion across Omani history. How
could it not be — it is the seedbed of Islam, the throne of the Arabs, the seat of the
Imamate, and the city of learning and of scholars. It is among the most productive Arab
cities in scholarship, and it holds an advanced place in the number of its scholars and
the volume of its books and its writing.

There were periods in Nizwa's history when not one of its houses was without a scholar in
it; a merchant, a porter or a builder might be enough to answer your question on a point
of learning, so many were the scholars in it.

For that reason it was celebrated in **2015** as Capital of Islamic Culture, and this
commemorative tower was raised for it on the frontage of the souq as a witness to it.

## The Mazara'a oven

Omanis were accustomed to roasting some of the meat of their slaughtered animals by
burying it in ovens fired with the wood of natural trees, on the days of Eid al-Fitr and
Eid al-Adha, in a fine social setting: the people of each quarter gather to make the Omani
*shuwa*. It is a social custom still alive today.

For some of these ovens the participants pay nothing at all, because they have endowments
of their own; the people take part in the work instead, and enjoy themselves in the doing.

## The grave of Sheikh Al Asamm

The grave of the learned Sheikh Al Asamm — Abu Abdullah Uthman bin Abdullah bin Ahmad
Al Azri — lies behind Al Shawadhna Mosque and west of Al Jalilain School. He was among the
leading scholars of the seventh century AH.

Wilayat Nizwa holds the graves of many Imams and scholars, but what is striking about this
Sheikh's grave is that it sits in the middle of the quarter — most scholars' and Imams'
graves being in the well-known cemetery west of the wilayat, and some on the edges of the
villages.

This Sheikh was not in fact deaf, as his byname suggests. There is a charming story behind
it, known to the people here: a woman came to him to ask a ruling, and in the course of
her question a sound of wind escaped her without her meaning it. The Sheikh, to spare her
the embarrassment, at once asked her to repeat her question — feigning inattention, with
his hand at his ear, as though his hearing were weak.

## The Persians' graveyard

This burial ground lies in a lane known as Sikkat Al Qabr — the lane of the grave — named
for a grave at its entrance. It is a mass grave for a large number of Persians.

They entered Oman in the last days of the Ya'ariba state, and entered Nizwa on **1 Dhu
al-Hijja 1150 AH**, and worked corruption in it. Their number was large — it is said
**four thousand** soldiers — and they came upon the town while its people were unaware.
They killed children and old men, seized women and violated what should have been
inviolable. The people therefore set an ambush for them, drawing them into the quarter and
shutting its gates so that they could not escape, and most of them were killed. The
country was spared their evil, and a great number of them were buried in this pit in one
large communal grave.

That has been the way of Omanis with whoever invaded them and took their land as lawful
plunder: no settlement until he is finished with and security restored to the country. It
is why some have called Oman the graveyard of invaders.

## Dr Thoms's hospital

The older people of Nizwa still remember the hospital of the American doctor Thoms — not
only because they were treated there, but because they preserve the memory of his treating
the Imam of Oman, **Muhammad bin Abdullah Al Khalili**, when blindness came upon his eyes
and God healed him through this doctor's care.

They also tell many stories and reports of him that they witnessed themselves, arising
from his deep admiration for the Imam's character. When he returned to his own country he
spoke to the clergy in America of his admiration for the Imam, saying that the Imam
represented the character of the Prophet Abraham. Sheikh Al Khalili related this himself.

## The water drainage channels

Since ancient times Omani engineering has taken account, in its building, of draining
rainwater through the quarters and between the houses in the lanes. So do not be surprised
to find these openings spread throughout the quarter. You may see rainwater running
through them, passing beneath the houses, until it empties into the channels of the *aflaj*
and the farms.

## The endowments (*awqaf*)

Omanis throughout Oman have been careful, since Islam came to them, to leave behind a
continuing charity, by endowing something of their property for a stated purpose. So one
finds a great many of the farms in the surrounding area are endowments for mosques,
schools, the poor, the needy, travellers, students of learning and teachers.

The endowments in Oman covered every need of the people, great and small. Some would even
endow household and sanitary items that many would think too cheap to bother with. Because
people held so firmly to this good custom, one might endow a palm tree from his property,
or a share of his portion of the *falaj*, or a room in his house for grinding grain, or
anything at all he judged would bring a return or a service people could benefit from. It
is said that the kinds of endowment in Harat Al Aqur alone come to **seventy**.

To protect the endowments, successive governments in Oman have long been careful to
appoint an honest and trustworthy trustee for every *waqf*, held to account by the
authorities on a continuing basis — work that goes on to this day.

Across history these endowments have been a cause of the spread of learning, of scholars,
of books and of libraries, and have been enough also to provide for the poor and the needy
in their lives.

## The wadi courses

Most Omani towns and villages are concentrated at the courses of the wadis that descend
from Oman's mountains in the north and the south; the wadi is the artery that feeds the
land with water.

In Nizwa, and through the middle of its souq, a group of large wadis passes, descending
from **Jabal Akhdar** and cutting tens of kilometres before they reach the centre of the
town. They divide the wilayat in two, the wadi cutting the road between the quarters of
Sa'al and Al Aqur.

When the wadis come down, they run for hours before slackening; the light flow then
continues for some days, forming beautiful rivers through the wilayat.

The best known of the wadis passing through Nizwa Souq is **Wadi Al Hajari**, the largest
and the fullest, which issues from the village of Tanuf; also **Wadi Al Musalla**,
**Al Suwaihriya**, **Kamah** and **Sumait**. Taken together, these wadis were formerly
called **Wadi Al Abyad** once they reached Nizwa Souq. They join **Wadi Kalbuh** just
before entering the souq, along with some other watercourses.

Wadi Kalbuh is among the most important wadis in Oman, given the many areas and wilayats
that benefit and are made fertile by its flow, because its sandy bed stores very large
quantities of groundwater.

### The drowning of Imam Al Warith bin Ka'b

This is the wadi in which the Imam of Oman, **Al Warith bin Ka'b Al Kharusi**, drowned, in
the well-known story.

Heavy rain fell, and Wadi Kalbuh ran hard; its level rose above the usual and its current
was strong. On the far bank was a prison holding a number of prisoners, and the Imam was
told the wadi would reach the prison and that they might drown. The Imam ordered them
released, but nobody dared cross the wadi. So the Imam said: they are my trust, and I shall
go to them myself, for I shall be asked about them on the Day of Judgement. He risked
himself, and the wadi swept him away in its force; it is said it swept away many of those
who followed him.

This is the character and the scruple of the Imams of Oman, and their fear for those in
their charge. Although the prisoners had been imprisoned for a crime they had committed,
the Imam never once thought of his own life, and lost it in consequence. The Imam's body
was found after the wadi fell, and he was buried in the same place. His grave is still
known today.

## Falaj Dawt

One of the old *aflaj* of Wilayat Nizwa, more than **twelve hundred years old** according
to the oldest mention found of it in the manuscripts.

The *aflaj* of the Sultanate divide into the *aini*, the *ghaili* and the *dawudi*. Across
Oman they are distinguished by remarkable engineering, by the manner of their digging, and
by the endurance and determination of the forefathers in cutting them from beneath the
ground over long distances. The channel of a *falaj* is sometimes found to run tens of
kilometres from its source to the first area it waters. Along these long channels are
openings distributed down their length, used during maintenance for air, for going down and
coming up, and for carrying out the spoil.

Despite the difficulty of digging in earlier times, with simple tools and in narrow, dark
channels, we find work of a refinement that baffles the mind and defeats description — and
through it we grasp the greatness of the Omanis and the strength of their will in bending
nature to their service.

This *falaj* is counted among the middling *aflaj* of Nizwa in force of flow. **Falaj
Daris** is the largest and the best known in Oman, and the stronger Falaj Daris runs, the
better it is for Falaj Dawt.

## The wells

No old Omani house is without a well of its own, used for drinking, bathing, cooking and
washing. These wells differ from farm wells in the narrowness of their bore: no more than
about one metre on average, whereas the bore of a farm well sometimes reaches three or four
metres, at a depth of up to twenty metres — less or more according to the water available
in that place.

The water of the wells and the *aflaj* is notable for being warm in winter and cool in
summer.

## The Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha celebrations

Nizwa Souq is at its best on the days of the two Eids, with popular celebrations, stalls
selling children's toys, and every kind of fast food made in the houses that you may not
find on any other day of the year.

And because Nizwa is a school for merchants, you will enjoy dealing with hundreds of
traders under the age of eighteen — most of the sellers being Omanis, and some of them
perhaps ten or eight years old. Even so you will find in them boldness, courage,
enthusiasm and real experience in dealing.

This is an old custom on this occasion, and most families come to it, bringing their sons
and their children with them. The children keep the time of the Eid from their earliest
years and would not trade it for anything else — delighted with the money they have made
from their Eid gift.

## Abu Al Mu'thir Museum

This museum sits above Sabah Abi Al Mu'thir, together with part of the Al Aqur Wall. A
visitor to it can try walking on the Al Aqur Wall and see the room that was used for the
watch, and climb one of the towers. He will also learn important historical information
and the old methods of guarding and defence.

## The *sa'at al-mudda* — the time clock

This is a clock used to measure the farmers' shares of the water of the *falaj*. It has
several measures: the **athar**, equal to half an hour; the **half-athar**, equal to a
quarter of an hour; and the **quarter-athar**, equal to seven and a half minutes.

It consists of a piece of wood from one of the trees, fixed in the ground, with the
measures set out around it. The clock is used from sunrise until sunset, by the shadow the
piece of wood casts on the ground.

At night the farmers' shares of the *falaj* water are measured by an instrument called the
**sahla**: a vessel made of copper with a small hole in the bottom. A large vessel is
brought, filled with water, and the *sahla* is placed empty on the surface, so that water
enters it through the hole beneath. When the *sahla* fills, the farmer has taken his share
of the *falaj*. There are several sizes of *sahla* too — the *athar*, the half and the
quarter.

The person who follows the clock and the measures is called the **arif al-falaj**; he is
responsible for distributing the *falaj* water among the farmers.

This clock stood in front of Al Jaljalan School for teaching the Qur'an, behind
Al Shawadhna Mosque. It belonged to Falaj Dawt — every *falaj* has its own method. We
could find no photograph of it; we found examples only in other wilayats. It disappeared
when its use stopped, and only its place remains.

## Nizwa Souq

Nizwa Souq is closely bound to Harat Al Aqur, the Friday Mosque and the pale fort. From of
old, Nizwa Castle, and then the fort — the two of them the political seat of the state —
were bound to bring into being a large principal souq for the region.

Nothing is known of the age of this souq except that it is among the most important Omani
markets, past and present. For its long standing and its purchasing power, many proverbs
were once coined about it. In **1992** it won an award for the best architectural and
engineering design.

What distinguishes it at present is that most of its sellers are Omanis. It is a souq in
which goods do not go unsold, however many and various they are, and it has room for
everyone to work in it, the young before the old. **Friday morning** is the ideal time to
visit, for its striking crowds and the variety of goods.

`[image]` *Nizwa Souq at night*

The most important of the small souqs belonging to Nizwa Souq, and the busiest and
liveliest at present:

### 1 — The eastern souq (Al Sansara)

A souq supplying every kind of spice and seasoning and aromatic, traditional Omani
remedies and local oils, together with some rare materials used in cooking, in farming and
in treatment. It also holds a great many items that are now sold nowhere else, because so
few people ask for them.

`[image]` *An old photograph of the Al Sansara souq from the western door*

### 2 — The silver and craftsmen's souq

Nizwa has been known since ancient times for working gold and silver, and keeps to it
today. The silver shops are grouped near the entrance of Nizwa Fort, with another group
beside the arms souq.

The most important thing these shops supply is the **Omani khanjar** in its various types
— the *nizwani* and the *sa'idi* (the *safani*) — along with the materials for making and
repairing them, and women's jewellery, traditional and modern, locally made and imported,
in different forms. They also offer a making and repair service for whatever customers ask.

### 3 — The gold souq

It lies north of the fort and west of the Friday Mosque, in the form of two strips divided
by the street. It is one of the most important gold markets in the Sultanate, serving every
governorate and supplying the different settings a woman might need, local or imported.

### 4 — The arms souq

This means the traditional weapons whose trade is permitted in the Sultanate. The souq
supplies these weapons together with ammunition and accessories, and a cleaning and
maintenance service. **Every Friday morning a public auction** is held for some of these
weapons, brought to the souq directly by their owners.

### 5 — The Friday market

An open souq for everyone, where you may offer whatever goods are permitted by law and by
custom. What is offered most are cars, furniture, household vessels, incense and clothes.
It begins in the first hours of Friday morning and runs to eleven o'clock.

### 6 — The dates and Omani halwa souq

Wilayat Nizwa is known for growing the finest date palms and for making Omani halwa. Its
souq is therefore full of stalls selling the halwa served in Omani hospitality with dates
and coffee. It also makes a fine gift, packed in handsome vessels that usually lean towards
Omani tradition. There are also stalls selling fresh and dried dates and their by-products
— *dibs*, *madluk* and *ma'mul*.

The souq further holds a great many of the goods an Omani household needs — meat, poultry,
fish, vegetables, fruit, aromatics, clothes and the rest — and has kept up old traditional
services, some of which have vanished from most markets. Examples:

- **The coppersmiths and blacksmiths** — who make and repair copper household vessels, and
  make and sharpen knives, swords and cleavers.
- **The jewellers** — who repair gold, silver and *khanjars*.
- **The cobbler (*iskafi*)** — who repairs and stitches shoes, and does leatherwork such as
  cutting sheaths for knives and some animal and agricultural requisites.

Beyond that are the various services now available in every city in the world — tailoring,
barbering, dressmaking, watch and electronics repair and so on.

### 7 — The livestock calling

Nizwa Souq holds goods of every kind, and a large part of them is sold by communal auction
on **Friday morning** of each week, and on the last days before Eid al-Fitr and Eid
al-Adha. Among these is the ground for selling sheep and cattle, known locally as the
*habta* and the *munada*. This ground is the best known in the Sultanate and the most
crowded.

Livestock is brought to it from across the wilayats of Oman, along with sellers coming in
from the nearby villages. Cattle come in numbers from the Governorate of Dhofar, and the
larger animals from the Governorate of North Al Batinah; a great many sheep come from the
governorates of Al Sharqiyah, Al Dhahirah and Al Wusta.

The auction begins at **seven in the morning** with the sale of sheep, then cattle, and
runs for about **three hours**, with dozens of brokers taking part at the same time. The
result is a fine spectacle, sought out by many from the wilayat and beyond. It is an old
custom in many of the principal Omani towns.

## Conclusion

By the praise of God and His help, this modest work is finished. What I wanted from it was
to acquaint the visitor with the history and the remains of Harat Al Aqur, in the city of
learning and of scholars, the seedbed of Islam — Nizwa — so that it might serve as a
tourist guide on his way around it.

Care has been taken to keep the material easy, clear, brief and concise, so that everyone
may read it without difficulty, and engaging, so that the reader does not tire of it.
Otherwise, to set out the history of this quarter properly would need many volumes.

This book will be translated into several world languages, with some changes and additions
in each printing to suit the language and the culture of that country, so that its
introduction may carry mention of the most notable historical relations between the two
countries. Its first printing will be, God willing, in **nine different languages**.
